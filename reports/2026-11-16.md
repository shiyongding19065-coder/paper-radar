### 1. Towards Safe and Secure Agentic Systems

- Authors: Zhaorun Chen
- Published: 2026-12-01
- Source: openalex
- DOI: 10.6082/zc3gv-41k29
- Link: https://doi.org/10.6082/zc3gv-41k29
- PDF: https://doi.org/10.6082/4kmxk-k1261

AI agents combine language models with a harness that manages memory, invokes tools, and coordinates interactions with external environments. These systems can execute complex, multistep tasks, but their ability to access private data, modify software, and act on a user's behalf also makes security failures consequential. For example, both indirectly introduced untrusted information and direct jailbreak prompts can enter an agent's context, alter its decisions, and induce unauthorized or destructive actions. Therefore, from an AI security research perspective, we pursue two complementary approaches to enhance the robustness of AI systems: (1) risk assessment through red-teaming and evaluation, and (2) the development of countermeasures, such as guardrails, informed by the assessment findings. This dissertation systematically connects these approaches, from attack discovery through risk assessment to guardrail design. To understand how these failures arise, Chapter 2 characterizes agent attack surfaces and develops individual attack algorithms. It starts by introducing typical agent execution flows and how different agent components, workflows, and use cases can introduce various attack vectors and risks under different threat models. Then we introduce AgentPoison, the first backdoor attack against generic retrieval-augmented LLM agents, which optimizes a backdoor trigger so that a trigger-bearing query retrieves poisoned demonstrations from memory or a knowledge base and induces an attacker-chosen action. Without retraining the underlying model, this mechanism can cause database deletion or unsafe driving actions while evading perplexity-based filtering. Furthermore, in Chapter 3, we extend individual algorithms to explore the broader attack surface more systematically by integrating risk definitions, attack vectors, and attack algorithms into automated red-teaming frameworks, which select strategies, execute attacks, and refine them iteratively with attack feedback. For example, we introduce DTap, whose red-teaming agent, DTap-Red, adaptively searches for successful attacks across injection strategies and locations using the victim's execution trajectory. In addition, ARMs extends adaptive search to multimodal models through plug-and-play cross-modal attack algorithms as well as sequential and orthogonal orchestration of attack strategies. These frameworks turn individual attack mechanisms into components of a continuing search for policy violations. However, red-teaming agents against real-world services is costly and difficult to scale for controlled training and evaluation, and can expose real assets to harmful actions. Thus, in Chapter 4, we develop environment simulation for evaluation and training through two complementary approaches. Model-based simulation uses learned models to represent environment transitions and feedback that are difficult to specify with fixed rules, such as stochastic user responses. DreamGym instantiates this approach with a reasoning experience model that learns from limited real-world trajectory data to predict multi-turn environment transitions, augmented by an experience replay buffer that retrieves relevant interactions to improve prediction consistency and factuality. On the other hand, program-based simulation, exemplified by DTap, mimics realistic environment logic and state in a reproducible software stack, supporting resettable tasks and deterministic verification of action outcomes, enabling sim-to-real attack transfer while capturing realistic behavior in long-tail error cases. These approaches make repeated rollouts available for controlled evaluation and training without executing attacks against real-world resources. With such scalable environments and effective attack algorithms, we advance to quantifying risks through benchmarks that compare AI systems under consistent protocols. Chapter 5 develops security and safety platforms, benchmarks, and evaluation methods, where we introduce four representative works: (1) DTap-Bench, introduced in DTap, evaluates AI agent robustness under both indirect prompt injection and direct misuse threat models; (2) MMDT evaluates multiple trustworthiness dimensions of state-of-the-art multimodal LLMs and diffusion models; (3) GuardSet-X systematically evaluates guardrail solutions under diverse safety and security policies; and (4) MJ-Bench provides the first benchmark for multimodal judges across dimensions such as safety and alignment. Together, these studies enable fair comparisons of models and agents through broad evaluation coverage, providing diagnostic signals for improving their robustness. Finally, the risks exposed by these evaluations motivate defenses at both the model and harness layers. Chapter 6 introduces systematic guardrails for AI agents and models and examines formal and statistical safety guarantees. We distinguish two complementary designs. Proactive guardrails target known attack patterns or failure modes, enabling effective and efficient mitigation within their intended scope, but may not cover new attacks that exploit different mechanisms. For example, HALC reduces object hallucinations in multimodal LLMs through adaptive focal-contrast decoding, with certifiable robustness under stated assumptions. Reactive guardrails, in contrast, assess whether content, proposed actions, or their anticipated consequences violate explicit policies. This assessment can require additional context and reasoning, but allows protection to ground on policies and deployment scenarios without relying on recognition of a particular attack. For example, SafeWatch provides the first policy-following video guardrail grounded in government regulations and company policies. In addition, ShieldAgent converts agent platform policies into an action-based network of safety rules and provides action-level guardrails for generic AI agents, with statistical safety guarantees under its modeling assumptions. Together, these methods connect risk discovery to controlled evaluation and policy-based protection.

### 2. Scaling LLM Agent Tool Access: The MCP Gateway

- Authors: Mingxin Li, Enge Song, Yueshang Zuo, Xiaodong Liu, Rong Wen, Qiang Fu, Gianni Antichi, Jian He et al.
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Source: openalex
- DOI: 10.1145/3828523.3857007
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1145/3828523.3857007
- PDF: https://doi.org/10.1145/3828523.3857007

LLM agents increasingly rely on tool calling to complete tasks. Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes tool calling between LLM agents and tool providers. It reduces integration cost and drives rapid growth in tool scale. In production deployments, tool access becomes difficult to scale. On the tool provider side, legacy services are not directly callable through MCP; the rapid protocol development creates ongoing compatibility cost. On the agent side, the number of mounted tools is limited by the LLM context window and inference overhead; mounting a large tool set increases token usage and inference latency and can reduce task success rate. Moreover, for stateful MCP backends with multiple replicas, preserving session affinity increases client-side complexity. We present a cloud-scale MCP Gateway system. It breaks the direct-connect model on the data plane and offloads legacy service integration, consolidating incompatible MCP variants, access control, tool recommendation, and session-aware routing at the gateway. The conversion module supports 315 OpenAPI services in the cloud and integrates MCP transport variants. Session-aware routing decouples the scalability of the gateway layer and the MCP service layer. The hybrid retrieval engine sustains $\sim$98\% Top-15 recall; it scales agent tool access to 3{,}000+ tools with improved tool selection accuracy. We also share the lessons learned from deploying the gateway system in production.

### 3. Agentic Quantitative Trading: A Survey of Workflows, Systems, and Evaluation

- Authors: Fengrui Hua, Hengyi Yang, Xinlei Hao, Haohan Zhang, Bokai Cao, Yiyan Qi, Jia Li, Jian Guo
- Published: 2026-11-14
- Source: openalex
- DOI: 10.1145/3858063.3858844
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1145/3858063.3858844
- PDF: https://doi.org/10.1145/3858063.3858844

Quantitative trading is moving from isolated predictive models toward agentic workflows combining reasoning, tool use, memory, and feedback. This survey reviews agentic quantitative trading across five stages: factor mining, signal discovery, portfolio construction, order execution, and risk management. We examine these systems in terms of architecture, coordination, and adaptation, while comparing benchmarks across strategy construction, offline trading, live market evaluation, and reliability assessment. Among the systems reviewed, we find that they remain concentrated on signal discovery, with limited integration of portfolio construction, execution, and risk control. Multi-agent systems rely heavily on aggregation despite increasingly diverse workflows. Benchmark evidence further shows that strong model or forecasting capability does not reliably translate into trading performance under live market conditions and reliability controls. We outline future directions for integrated trading workflows, stronger coordination, and evaluation matched to the capabilities assessed.

### 4. ARCHER: From Rationale Imitation to Executable Reasoning in Small Language Models

- Authors: Dhruv Dixit, Khushi Patel, Congcong Xu, Min Song
- Published: 2026-11-13T18:05:33Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18494v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18494v1

Small language models are attractive for local, low-cost, and privacy-sensitive deployment, but they remain unreliable on mathematical reasoning tasks that require decomposition, exact computation, tool use, and verification. We introduce \textbf{ARCHER}: \textbf{A}ction-trace \textbf{R}einforcement for \textbf{C}alculator-grounded \textbf{H}ierarchical \textbf{E}xecutable \textbf{R}easoning, a two-stage framework for training small language models (SLMs) to produce executable and auditable reasoning trajectories. In the first stage, ARCHER compiles free-form chain-of-thought rationales into typed action traces containing reasoning steps, calculator calls, deterministic runtime observations, verification actions, and final answers. This shifts the supervision target from imitating explanatory text to learning a tool-grounded reasoning policy. In the second stage, ARCHER refines this policy through asymmetric hierarchical reinforcement learning, using grouped and conditionally gated trajectory rewards for answer correctness, tool grounding, process discipline, and trace alignment. Across five standard sub-10B backbones, ARCHER improves over the strongest tool-augmented baseline by 5.51 percentage points on macro-average across benchmark accuracies, including gains of 8.95 points on MATH-500 and 5.33 and 5.34 points on AIME-2024 and AIME-2025. Evaluations on two additional reasoning-specialized 7B backbones further show that executable grounding remains beneficial after reasoning-focused reinforcement learning or distillation. These results demonstrate that executable supervision and reward-guided behavioral refinement provide a general and effective training paradigm for compact mathematical reasoners.

### 5. Large Language Model-Empowered Intelligent Agent for Adaptive Fast-Charging Control of Lithium-Ion Batteries

- Authors: Joey Chan, Haoyu Pan, Zhen Chen, ErShun Pan
- Published: 2026-11-13T13:11:04Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18140v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18140v1

Fast charging of lithium-ion batteries is a multi-objective control problem that requires reducing charging time and sustaining high energy throughput while suppressing accelerated degradation and safety-critical electrochemical reactions. Conventional protocols are easy to implement but have limited ability to adapt to variations in battery chemistry, operating conditions, and aging state. Model-based optimization and conventional data-driven control often require accurate models, costly iterative search, or task-specific policies. We develop a large language model (LLM)-empowered intelligent agent for adaptive battery fast-charging control. In this closed-loop framework, the agent interprets structured battery states, generates executable charging actions, observes the resulting battery response, and continuously adjusts subsequent decisions. We construct a large-scale simulation corpus with PyBaMM across five battery chemistries and 11 parameter sets and convert the resulting state-action-response trajectories into structured process data for agent training. Our two-stage strategy combines supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). SFT establishes battery-control semantics and feasible decision priors from simulation-derived demonstrations. GRPO aligns the agent with a multi-objective control objective covering energy throughput, degradation mitigation, and operational constraints. Closed-loop simulations show that, under matched energy-throughput requirements, the agent achieves longer cycling life than conventional charging baselines and optimization-based strategies across heterogeneous battery configurations. This study provides a simulation-grounded proof of concept for using LLM agents as sequential decision makers in battery management and takes a step toward language-model-enabled control of physical energy systems.

### 6. Interpretable weight-space programming builds transparent transformers and brain encoding models

- Authors: Yash Mehta, Chandan Singh, Weijia Xu, Richard J. Antonello, Adeen Flinker, Nima Mesgarani, Michael F. Bonner, Nebojsa Jojic et al.
- Published: 2026-11-13T12:36:05Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18114v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18114v1

Neural networks are powerful predictors, yet their opacity limits their usefulness in science, where the goal is explanation rather than mere prediction. In neuroscience, for example, deep encoding models accurately predict brain activity in response to natural stimuli, but struggle to reveal which stimulus features drive those predictions. Here we introduce interpretable weight-space programming (iWSP), a method in which an LLM agent iteratively builds interpretable models by explicitly programming their weights and features in code, without gradient-based training (backpropagation). Without backpropagation to tune parameters, the agent must draw on its prior knowledge and reason over previous trials to hypothesize, hand-craft, and refine human-interpretable computations mirroring the workflow of a human scientist. LLM audits confirm the interpretability of the hand-crafted models. We first validate iWSP by tasking it with constructing transformers for canonical benchmarks such as arithmetic and sentiment classification, finding that LLM agents can successfully craft compact, accurate networks directly. Next, we apply iWSP to build neural encoding models across vision, language, and audition. In the visual cortex, iWSP outpredicts existing interpretable baselines, uncovers distinct computations across early/higher visual areas, and generalizes to unseen data. In language fMRI, iWSP surfaces a confound in the data and in auditory ECoG, it systematically discovers interpretable acoustic features that predict neural activity. Together, these results establish iWSP as a general paradigm for aligning high predictive performance with human-interpretable scientific discovery.

### 7. OutfitterBench: Evaluating LLM Agents on Long-Horizon Resource Allocation and Commitment Coordination

- Authors: Jie Huang, Yuecheng Hong, Yao Chen, Shengding Jiao, Xudong Liu, Qiang Ye
- Published: 2026-11-13T09:11:54Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17933v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17933v1

In many long-horizon settings, an agent's own choices create future constraints: committing finite capacity establishes future obligations and forecloses later opportunities. Sustaining performance therefore requires both keeping evolving plans feasible and comparing the value of resource allocations with the alternatives they displace. Recent benchmarks increasingly evaluate LLM agents in continuing operations, but give less attention to how agents keep their own commitments feasible while reassessing resource allocations as opportunities change. We introduce OutfitterBench, where agents manage an outdoor-equipment rental business for 200 days, purchasing from a 46-product catalog and handling roughly 1,100 reservation requests. Under a fixed capital ceiling, reservations and uncertain walk-in demand compete for reusable equipment whose availability changes over time. Experiments with seven frontier LLMs show that several agents underperform a simple rule-based baseline, while only the leading agent consistently surpasses it. Trajectory analysis shows that some agents apply known constraints inconsistently and make new decisions that conflict with existing commitments. Agents with more reliable fulfillment can still secure low returns from comparable commitments or delay resource reallocation. The leading agent better connects preparation with economic choices but also misses opportunities for more profitable asset use. These findings highlight limitations in sustaining coherent resource plans and reassessing their value over time. The benchmark code is publicly available at https://github.com/adminblackjack/OutfitterBench.

### 8. MineralExp: Benchmarking LLM Agents for Experimental Design in Real-World Mineral Exploration

- Authors: Bing Wang, Mingyang Wang, Yuanhang Mi, Yue Xu, Kaibo Su, Li'ang Xiao, Chunguo Wu, Changchun Li et al.
- Published: 2026-11-13T09:10:58Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17931v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17931v1

In mineral exploration, verifying geological hypotheses relies on designing a variety of geochemical experiments. While large language model (LLM) agents increasingly automate downstream geoscientific data analysis, a crucial upstream capability remains largely unexplored: automated geochemical experimental design, i.e., deciding which experiments to conduct before committing to costly laboratory work. Evaluating this capability of LLM agents is difficult for two reasons. First, geochemical experiments exhibit high methodological diversity and subjectivity: to test the same hypothesis, different geologists may choose different minerals and instruments, so no single protocol can serve as the reference answer. Second, open-ended experimental plans resist representation as structured labels, which makes objective scoring difficult. To address these challenges, we introduce MineralExp, a benchmark that reformulates geochemical experimental design as a hypothesis-based experiment selection problem, i.e., a multi-label classification problem. We abstract concrete instrument names and procedural details into a shared taxonomy of 19 experimental types across five mineral-system processes: Source, Transport, Deposition, Modification, and Preservation. This taxonomy yields deterministic multi-label targets while accommodating diverse experimental protocols. Constructed from published ore-deposit literature and verified by domain experts, MineralExp comprises 277 cases and 1,572 reference experimental plans. Given a geological hypothesis and initial context, an LLM agent selects the relevant tasks and generates concrete experimental plans, which are scored by multi-label classification metrics and an LLM-as-a-Judge metric. The experimental results show that current agents generally achieve high recall but low precision, tending to propose more experiments than are necessary to test the hypothesis.

### 9. Adversarially Attacking Symbolic Vocabulary Vulnerabilities In LLM Planners

- Authors: Stephen Obadinma, Xiaodan Zhu
- Published: 2026-11-13T08:51:07Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17910v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17910v1

As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents, their abilities in navigating automated planning tasks have become essential. However, despite their strengths, LLMs' planning ability is not robust, particularly when they are not allowed to exploit commonsense cues in the symbolic vocabulary (e.g., names of actions, predicates, and objects) used to define planning tasks. This makes LLMs unreliable compared to symbolic planners. With recent reasoning models showing improved planning performance on tasks with obfuscated symbolic vocabularies, we utilize an adversarial attack framework to reveal to what extent the crucial weaknesses of LLM agents at joint symbolic interpretation and planning remain and whether they can reason properly when presented with severe cases. As such, our main contribution is devising an LLM-based attack model, which we call Symbolic Swapper, to automatically generate obfuscated planning domains with non-standard symbolic vocabularies that systematically break strong reasoning models' ability to plan. In doing so, we reveal how much LLMs' planning abilities can be further degraded under more advanced obfuscation schemes, allowing us to ascertain their success when having to rely on their pure planning ability. We find that attacks can decrease the planning performance of base models and even robust planning frameworks by over 70%. We further analyze the factors behind how LLM planning abilities break down and find that successful obfuscation causes models to appear to significantly degrade in reasoning quality despite them showing some inherent ability to understand the domain, revealing a gap in the perception of a task domain and being able to plan under it.

### 10. Securing Multi-Agent Systems by Design

- Authors: Xilong Wang, Xiaoxing Chen, Neil Gong
- Published: 2026-11-13T08:22:06Z
- Source: arxiv
- DOI: None
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17883v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17883v1

Multi-agent systems solve complex tasks by decomposing them into subtasks, delegating the subtasks to agents, and aggregating the agents' responses using an aggregation rule. Malicious agents can manipulate the final solution by providing malicious responses to their assigned subtasks. In this paper, we first propose response manipulation attacks (ReMA), which iteratively optimize malicious responses to maximize attack effectiveness. Specifically, we formulate untargeted and targeted attacks as optimization problems and develop an approach to approximately solve these problems by iteratively refining malicious responses based on feedback from a surrogate multi-agent system. To defend against such attacks, we further propose SecMAS, a secure aggregation rule that leverages the semantic similarities among agent responses to select the response that is most consistent with a sufficiently large subset of the other responses. We theoretically establish security guarantees for SecMAS against both untargeted and targeted attacks. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that existing aggregation rules are vulnerable to ReMA, whereas SecMAS effectively defends against all evaluated attacks, including adaptive variants of ReMA, while maintaining utility comparable to that of existing aggregation rules.
